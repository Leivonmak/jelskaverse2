## Physical Presence

**Height:** Medium build, perhaps 178–180 cm. Thinning brown hair, close-cropped. Blue-grey eyes — pale, direct, and evaluating. Short, deliberate stubble that reads as "too busy to shave every morning" rather than a stylistic choice. Strong jaw, weathered face. The face of a man who has spent time in deserts and in basements, and who is completely comfortable in both.

**Bearing:** Still. Not the theatrical stillness of a man performing composure — the authentic stillness of a man who learned decades ago that unnecessary movement is unnecessary information. He listens more than he speaks. When he does speak, he is precise. He does not waste words because he was trained in an environment where wasted words could get people killed.

**Dress:** As of January 1991, UOP service uniform — olive green, functional, unadorned except for rank insignia. He does not dress up. He does not dress down. He wears what the institution requires and does not think about it further.

**Age in photographs:** He looks older than he is. The Sinai aged him. The SB aged him more. He is 37 but could pass for 45. This is an asset — colleagues and counterparts consistently overestimate his age, which means they consistently overestimate his seniority. Nobody questions a 45-year-old colonel. A 37-year-old colonel invites questions.

## Education & Formation

### Stare Kiejkuty — Intelligence School
*Date TBD, approximately early 1970s*

The Zarząd II Sztabu Generalnego (Second Directorate of the General Staff — Polish military intelligence under the communist system) maintained its primary training facility at Stare Kiejkuty, a remote military compound in the Masurian lake district. This is where the People's Republic of Poland trained its intelligence officers: tradecraft, agent handling, dead drops, surveillance and counter-surveillance, interrogation techniques, signals intelligence fundamentals, and the ideological framing that justified all of it.

Syrop graduated from Stare Kiejkuty as a fully trained military intelligence officer of the Polish People's Republic. He learned the communist system's methods from the inside. He was good at them. He was better than most of his classmates. This was noticed — upward and, eventually, westward.

### "Kurs Specjalny Paryż" — The Turning
*Approximately mid-to-late 1970s*

"Kurs Specjalny Paryż" is a codename — not a training program. It designates a Polish intelligence deployment: Syrop was sent to France by the Zarząd II / AWO as an **operational agent**, tasked with spying on French targets. "Paryż" is Paris. He was a PRL spy deployed to France.

He was caught. Or rather — he was *identified*, assessed, and approached. The DGSE (or its predecessor, SDECE) detected him, studied his profile, and made a determination: this man can be turned. Not through blackmail, not through ideology — through an offer that aligned with something he already believed but had never been given the framework to act on.

The specifics of the turning are compartmentalized. What is known:

- **He was deployed to France** as part of a Zarząd II operation — likely industrial or scientific espionage targeting French defense or technology sectors, which were primary AWO targets in the 1970s.
- **French counter-intelligence identified him.** This is not unusual — the DST (Direction de la Surveillance du Territoire, French domestic counter-intelligence) routinely identified Eastern Bloc intelligence officers operating on French soil.
- **Instead of arresting or expelling him, the DGSE turned him.** They offered him the pitch that The Technocracy has refined over decades: you are not betraying Poland. You are serving the *real* Poland — the one that existed before the communists, the one that broke Enigma and shared it at Pyry, the one that will exist again when the system falls. The communist regime is temporary. What we are building is permanent.
- **He accepted.** He returned to Warsaw as a double agent — ostensibly reporting to the Zarząd II on his French operations, actually operating under DGSE/Technocracy direction. His Polish superiors received enough genuine intelligence to consider the deployment a success. The DGSE received everything else.

**What the turning gave Syrop:** French intelligence tradecraft (which differs from Soviet-derived Polish tradecraft in specific, important ways), an understanding of Western communications security, direct personal contact with DGSE officers who would later become Silberzahn's inner circle, and — most importantly — a second loyalty that he carried back into the Polish intelligence apparatus and maintained, undetected, for over a decade.

The name "Kurs Specjalny" (Special Course) is darkly ironic — in Warsaw's files, it reads like a routine training designation. In reality, it was a course in becoming someone else while remaining exactly who you are.

## Career Path

### Agenturalny Wywiad Operacyjny (AWO) — 1974–1981
*Operational Agent Intelligence of the Polish Army*

The AWO was the Polish Army's field intelligence arm — the unit that ran human intelligence operations, managed agent networks, and conducted operational espionage. Not the analysts. Not the signals people. The officer handlers, the recruiters, the men who sat in cars outside embassies and met contacts in parks after dark.

Syrop spent seven years in the AWO doing exactly this work. He was good at it. His language skills (already developing — Polish, Russian, and French from Paris, English improving through operational contact with NATO-country targets) made him unusually versatile. Most AWO officers spoke Polish and Russian. Syrop could operate across half the world.

### UNEF II — Sinai Deployment
*During AWO service, approximately 1975–1977*

Poland contributed a logistics battalion to the United Nations Emergency Force II, deployed in the Sinai Peninsula to monitor the ceasefire between Egypt and Israel after the 1973 Yom Kippur War. Syrop was deployed as a military intelligence officer embedded within the Polish contingent — officially a logistics liaison, actually collecting intelligence on Egyptian military capabilities, Israeli operational patterns, and the broader Middle Eastern theater.

**This is where he learned Arabic.** Not fluently at first — functional Arabic acquired through daily operational necessity in the Sinai, later refined to B2 through continued study and occasional operational use. The Arabic capability is rare in Polish intelligence and makes him one of a handful of Polish officers who can operate in the Middle Eastern theater without a translator.

**This is also where he first encountered the Israeli military intelligence apparatus.** UNEF II operated in the buffer zone between Egypt and Israel. Israeli military intelligence (Aman) monitored the UN force closely. Syrop, as an intelligence officer embedded in the force, had professional contact — guarded, formal, but real — with Israeli counterparts. The experience gave him an understanding of Israeli operational culture that very few Polish officers possess.

**Connection to the extended network:** Syrop's Sinai experience and Arabic capability are directly relevant to The Technocracy's Oman node. An intelligence officer who speaks Arabic and has operational experience in the Middle East is an asset that The Technocracy can deploy in contexts that most European intelligence officers cannot navigate.

### Transfer to SB — Departament I MSW — 1981–1990
*Interior Ministry, First Department (Foreign Intelligence)*

In 1981, Syrop was transferred from military intelligence (AWO/Zarząd II) to the civilian security service — the Służba Bezpieczeństwa (SB), specifically its First Department (Departament I MSW), which handled foreign intelligence operations.

This transfer is significant in several ways:

1. **It gave him access to the civilian intelligence apparatus.** AWO/Zarząd II was military; Departament I was civilian. By serving in both, Syrop acquired institutional knowledge of *the entire Polish intelligence landscape* — he knows how both halves work, who the key people are in both structures, and where the rivalries and gaps lie. This is exceptionally rare and exceptionally valuable.

2. **Departament I was the foreign intelligence arm.** Not domestic surveillance (that was other departments). Departament I ran Poland's foreign intelligence operations — spies abroad, agent networks in Western countries, intelligence collection on NATO targets. This is the department that would have had the most contact with allied (Soviet, East German, Czech) and occasionally adversarial (Western) intelligence services.

3. **He continued operating under Technocracy direction throughout.** The SB did not know that one of its First Department officers was a Western asset. Syrop maintained his cover for nine years inside the most paranoid institution in communist Poland. This required extraordinary discipline, complete compartmentalization of his dual loyalties, and the kind of professional competence that made his superiors trust him completely precisely because he was, in every visible metric, an exemplary officer.

4. **He knows where the bodies are buried.** Nine years in the SB's foreign intelligence department means Syrop knows every Polish intelligence operation of the 1980s — the successes, the failures, the embarrassments, the crimes. He knows which officers were competent and which were ideological hacks. He knows which agents abroad are still active and which were burned. He knows the filing systems, the archive locations, the classification protocols. This institutional knowledge is, in January 1991, one of the most valuable assets in Poland.

### Lustration — 1990
*Verification for democratic service*

When the SB was dissolved and replaced by the UOP (May 1990), all former SB officers underwent a lustration process — a verification to determine whether they were suitable for service in the new democratic state. Officers who had committed abuses, who were considered ideologically compromised, or who were deemed security risks were dismissed.

Syrop passed.

**How he passed** is a question with multiple possible answers:

- **He was genuinely clean.** His SB work was in foreign intelligence (Departament I), not domestic repression. He did not participate in surveillance of dissidents, did not torture prisoners, did not run operations against Solidarity. His hands are, in the specific legal sense that lustration examined, clean. This is plausible — Departament I officers often had a very different profile from the internal security departments.
- **He was protected.** The Technocracy — through Krzysztof, through Silberzahn's channels, through contacts in the verification process — ensured that Syrop's file was handled favorably. Not falsified — that would be too risky — but framed in a way that emphasized his Departament I foreign intelligence work and minimized any uncomfortable associations.
- **Both.** He was genuinely clean AND was protected. The best cover is the truth, selectively presented.

### UOP — Chief of the Office for State Protection (Szef UOP) — June 1991
*Urząd Ochrony Państwa (Office for State Protection)*

As of June 1991, Syrop is appointed the **Chief of the Office for State Protection (Szef UOP)**. He takes over the leadership of this brand-new institution that is still defining its own structure, procedures, and mission. The UOP is one year old. It inherited some SB personnel (the ones who passed lustration), some physical infrastructure, and an enormous burden of institutional mistrust — the Polish public does not trust any intelligence service, regardless of what it calls itself, because the SB's legacy of domestic repression is still fresh.

Syrop's position as Chief of the UOP gives him:

- **Access to strategic decision-making and operational planning** at the supreme level
- **Direct authority over personnel decisions** — who gets promoted, who gets assigned to which department, who is trusted with sensitive operations
- **Institutional knowledge** that most of his colleagues lack — he has served in both military and civilian intelligence, he has operational experience abroad (Sinai), he has foreign intelligence expertise (Departament I), and he speaks six languages
- **Credibility with both old-guard and new-guard factions** — the former SB officers respect his competence and seniority; the new democratic appointees respect his clean lustration record and his willingness to serve the new system

He is, in short, exactly where The Technocracy needs him: leading the new Polish intelligence establishment, at the highest level to shape its development and prepare for transition to the KBP/ABK architecture, trusted enough by both sides to operate without suspicion.

## Character Profile

### Core disposition

Syrop is not Krzysztof. Krzysztof is the architect — the man who sees the whole board and moves pieces across it. Syrop is the **operator** — the man who makes things happen at the institutional level, inside the machinery of the state, where policy meets practice and where the difference between a plan and a reality is a hundred small decisions made by one competent person in the right position.

He does not think in civilizational timescales. He thinks in operational cycles: what needs to happen this week, this month, this quarter, for the larger plan to remain on track. He trusts Krzysztof's vision without fully understanding all of it — he knows the Polish operation (Foreuse/Świder), he knows there is a larger architecture (the Axis), but he does not know the full scope of The Technocracy's global ambitions. He doesn't need to. He needs to know what to do in the UOP tomorrow morning, and he does.

### His relationship with the communist past

Complicated, unsentimentalized. He served the communist system for nearly twenty years — not because he believed in it, but because the system was the environment he operated in, and the Technocracy needed him inside it. He does not feel guilt about his SB service because he was never ideologically communist — he was always working toward the system's eventual replacement. But he does not romanticize his position either. He did things in Departament I that served Soviet interests, even as he was secretly serving Western ones. The moral ledger does not balance cleanly, and he knows it.

He will not discuss this with anyone. If asked about his SB years, he gives the factual minimum required and changes the subject.

### His relationship with Krzysztof

**Loyalty, not friendship.** Syrop respects Krzysztof as the architect and recognizes his strategic superiority. Their relationship is professional — Krzysztof gives direction, Syrop executes. There is mutual respect but not intimacy. Krzysztof is a wealthy, French-educated émigré who has never lived under the system Syrop spent two decades navigating from the inside. The experiential gap between them is vast, and both know it.

Syrop does not resent this gap. He recognizes that Krzysztof's distance from the system is precisely what makes him effective — an architect who has never been contaminated by the institution he is redesigning. But he occasionally thinks, privately, that Krzysztof underestimates how difficult it is to change an institution from the inside, and how many compromises that requires.

*(Note: How and when they first met — TBD. Likely through the Paris channel or through Silberzahn. The connection predates 1989.)*

### His relationship with the UOP

Institutional. He believes in the UOP's mission — building a democratic intelligence service for a democratic Poland — and he works toward it with genuine commitment. He also knows that the UOP as currently constituted is fragile, underfunded, undertrained, and politically exposed. It is a seven-month-old institution trying to do the work of a mature intelligence service in a country surrounded by threats, and it is doing so with a staff half-composed of former SB officers whom the public does not trust and half-composed of new hires who do not yet know what they're doing.

Syrop's value to the UOP is precisely that he bridges these two halves. He is former SB (institutional knowledge, tradecraft, networks) but lustration-clean (legitimacy, democratic credentials). He can walk into a meeting with old-guard officers and speak their language, then walk into a meeting with the new democratic appointees and speak theirs. He is the institutional translator.

### His view of the future

He believes Poland needs a strong intelligence service — stronger than the UOP currently is, more professional, more capable, less dependent on political patronage. He believes this because he has seen what happens when intelligence services are weak (the SB was politically powerful but operationally mediocre in many areas) and what happens when they are strong (the Mossad and DGSE, which he has observed from adjacent positions). He wants to build the latter in Poland.

Whether the institution he builds will serve Polish democracy or The Technocracy's vision of a spy-state is a question he does not ask himself in those terms. He would say: a strong, professional intelligence service serves Poland. The fact that it also serves The Technocracy's interests is, in his framing, not a contradiction but a convergence. Both want a strong Poland. The means are the same even if the ultimate ends differ.

## Notes for Roleplay (Admin-Level UOP)

- **He is the most competent person in most rooms.** Not the most senior, not the most politically connected — the most competent. This gives him informal authority that exceeds his formal rank.
- **He does not volunteer information.** He answers questions precisely and completely. He does not elaborate unless asked. He does not speculate. He reports what he knows, what he doesn't know, and what he recommends.
- **He is respected by former SB colleagues and tolerated by new appointees.** Neither group fully trusts him — the old guard suspects he is too accommodating to the new system, and the new appointees suspect he is too comfortable with the old one. He operates in the gap between these suspicions.
- **His six languages are operational tools.** He switches between them without ceremony — if a document is in Russian, he reads it in Russian and summarizes in Polish. If a foreign contact calls, he answers in their language. This is not performed; it is automatic.
- **He carries a notebook.** Small, leather-bound, in his inner jacket pocket. He writes in it in a personal shorthand that combines Polish, Russian, and French. Nobody else can read it. This is deliberate.
- **He does not drink socially.** Not out of principle — out of operational discipline. He has seen too many officers compromise themselves over vodka. He will hold a glass if the situation requires it. He will not finish it.
- **He sleeps four hours a night and has done so for twenty years.** He is not proud of this. It is simply the rhythm his body settled into during the Sinai and never left.
